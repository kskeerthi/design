01  How I work

Three things I believe about good experience.

Start with the person, not the interface.

The best design usually looks like nothing happened. Before I draw a single screen I want to know who is using it, what they are afraid of getting wrong, and what a good day looks like for them. Everything else follows from that.

Make the machine explain itself.

People do not trust answers they cannot check. On AI products I design so the system shows its reasoning and keeps the real data in view. The idea I keep coming back to is simple. The AI reads and explains, the data decides.

Ship, then learn.

A design in someone's hands teaches you more in a week than a deck teaches you in a month. I move fast to something real, watch what happens, and improve it. Speed and care are not opposites when the loop is tight.

These two lines are exactly the same length.

Your eyes still pick one. It is why I trust what people do over what they say, and why I watch closely before I design a single screen.

02  Selected work

A few things I have designed and led.

Screens that have to work for millions of strangers

UI and UX, self-service at NCR

NCR is one of the companies behind the ATMs and self-order kiosks you use without a second thought. I designed for both, the banking terminals people trust with their money and the self-order screens at counters like McDonald's. A public screen like that is used by an enormous number of people in a single day, and you do not get to choose who they are.

The discipline is unforgiving. There is no onboarding, no help desk, no second try before someone gives up and walks away. It simply has to be obvious. Someone using a wheelchair, someone who cannot see the screen well, someone who has never touched a touchscreen, someone in a rush with a child on their hip, all of them have to get it right on the first attempt.

So I designed for reach and posture, for low vision and colour, for people who rely on audio, and for anyone meeting the machine cold. Accessibility here was never a feature bolted on at the end. It was the entire brief. If it does not work for the person the system usually forgets, it does not work. That is the same standard a health experience has to meet, where the people who need it most are often the ones a system serves worst.

The challenge

A public screen you cannot explain to anyone

Most software gets to introduce itself. There is an onboarding, a help centre, a colleague to ask. A self-service terminal has none of that. Someone walks up, often in a hurry, sometimes stressed, and the machine has one chance to make sense before they give up or ask a staff member for help, which defeats the whole point of self-service.

And you do not get to choose your users. An ATM or a McDonald's kiosk is used by the entire public. Different ages, languages, abilities, and comfort with technology, all in the same day, at the same screen, with no tutorial. The bar is not works for most people. It is works for whoever walks up next.

Who it is for

Everyone, which really means the edges

When you design for everyone, the people who decide whether you succeeded are the ones at the edges. The person in a wheelchair who cannot reach the top of the screen. The person with low vision who cannot read small grey text under glare. The person who has never used a touchscreen and does not know a flat rectangle is a button. The person whose first language is not the default on the screen.

Designing for them is not charity, it is rigour. If the experience holds up for the person the system usually forgets, it holds up for everybody.

How I approached it

Watch first, then design for the worst case

I started where the machines actually live, not at a desk. Watching people use self-service in the wild tells you things no requirements document will. Where they hesitate, what they tap by mistake, when they give up and look for a human. The failures are the brief.

From there I designed against the hardest conditions rather than the easiest. Bright light on the screen, a noisy room, a short person and a tall person and a seated person all needing the same controls, someone who will only ever use this once. If it works under those conditions, the easy cases take care of themselves.

Key design decisions

Reach and posture

Controls placed and sized so a seated or wheelchair user can reach everything that matters, not just the middle of the screen.

Legible under glare

High contrast, generous type, and colour that still reads in sunlight, not just in a design tool.

Obvious touch targets

Big, clearly tappable elements with room to spare, so a shaky hand or a first-timer does not mis-tap.

Audio and non-visual paths

A way through for people who cannot rely on the screen alone, so the experience is not locked behind sight.

Plain, calm language

Words a stranger understands instantly, so the screen never makes someone feel slow.

Forgiving flows

Easy to go back, hard to get stuck, clear recovery when something goes wrong. Nobody feels trapped.

Looking back

Why this still shapes how I work

Accessibility is not a stage near the end of a project. It is the whole discipline of designing for the person the system usually forgets, and it makes the product better for everyone along the way. That instinct matters more than ever now, because AI experiences, voice, agents, chat, all carry the same risk of quietly working for the confident majority and failing everyone else. In a health experience the stakes are higher still, because the people who need it most are exactly the ones a careless system serves worst.

One design system, many products shipped fast

Built and led with my UI and UX team

A good design system is the difference between shipping one nice thing and shipping ten. With my UI and UX team I built the design system we now use to stand up POCs quickly, a shared library of components, patterns and rules so every new idea starts from something solid instead of a blank canvas.

It means our POCs look and feel considered from the first screen, the team moves faster, and the experience stays consistent no matter who is building which part. That is the real leverage of a lead. You stop designing screens one at a time and start designing the thing that lets a whole team design well. It is exactly how one experience lead keeps many POC teams pulling in the same direction.

The challenge

A small team, and far more ideas than hands

In an innovation team the demand is endless. Every week there is another idea that needs to become a working POC, fast enough to show and solid enough to be taken seriously. Start each one from a blank canvas and two things happen. You are slow, because everyone rebuilds the same buttons and forms every time. And you are inconsistent, because ten POCs by different people end up looking like ten different companies made them.

Neither is acceptable when the whole point is to move quickly and look credible. The bottleneck was not talent or ideas. It was that the team kept starting from zero.

The insight

Stop designing screens, design the thing that makes screens

The shift was to treat the system itself as the product. Instead of designing another beautiful one-off, put the effort into a shared foundation that every future POC can stand on. Do the hard thinking once, about spacing, colour, type, components, states and accessibility, and then let everyone draw from it.

"You stop designing screens one at a time and start designing the thing that lets a whole team design well."

How I built it

Audit what exists

Gather the patterns scattered across past work, find the duplication, decide what to keep, merge or drop.

Foundations first

Set the tokens underneath everything, colour, type scale, spacing, so it is consistent by construction, not by policing.

Components with real states

Not just the happy path. Loading, empty, error, disabled, so a POC handles reality without reinventing it.

Accessibility built in

Contrast, focus and target sizes baked into the components, so every POC inherits accessibility.

Made to be adopted

Clear enough to use without me in the room. A system nobody understands is just a folder of files.

Living, not frozen

It grows as the team hits new needs, so it stays useful instead of going stale after launch.

Why it matters for a lead role

This is what leading the experience looks like

A design system is leadership made tangible. It is how one person keeps many teams pulling in the same direction without standing over every screen. When several POC teams build in parallel, the system is what makes the pieces add up to one coherent product instead of a pile of clever fragments. That is exactly the shape of an end to end experience lead role. You are not the person who designs everything, you are the person who makes it possible for everyone to design well, and consistently, at speed.

Ask it a question, or let the dashboard tell you first

Experience lead, AI knowledge system

Teams know a great deal, but it lives in scattered documents, threads and heads. I led the experience for an AI knowledge system with two ways in. A chat where you ask a question the way you would ask a colleague and get an answer with its sources attached. And dashboards that surface the things worth knowing before you even think to ask.

The chat is for the question you already have. The dashboards are for the question you did not know to ask, the trends, the gaps, the things that need attention, laid out so a busy person can read the state of things in a glance and go deeper only where it matters. One system, two speeds, depending on how much someone already knows they are looking for.

The hard part was never the search or the charts. It was making the whole thing feel accountable. The design earns trust by always showing where an answer or a number came from, which is the whole game for AI in a regulated, high-stakes world where a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer at all.

The challenge

A team that knows everything, and can find nothing

Any established team is sitting on an enormous amount of knowledge. The trouble is where it lives, scattered across documents, chat threads, decks, and people's heads. When someone needs an answer they either interrupt a colleague or spend an afternoon digging, and half the time the thing they find is out of date.

AI can obviously help. But dropping a chatbot on a pile of documents creates a worse problem. An AI that answers confidently but cannot show its work is dangerous, because people will believe it, and in a serious setting a confident wrong answer costs more than no answer at all.

The core idea

Two ways in, for two kinds of question

People arrive at knowledge in two different states, and the design respects both. The chat is for the question you already have, asked in your own words, answered with its sources attached so you can check it in one tap. The dashboards are for the question you did not know to ask, the trends and gaps and things that need attention, laid out so a busy person can read the state of things at a glance and dig deeper only where it matters.

"One system, two speeds, depending on how much someone already knows they are looking for."

Designing for trust

Sources, always

Every answer and every number carries where it came from, one tap away, so people verify instead of taking it on faith.

Honest about certainty

The design signals when the system is sure and when it is not, so tone matches the facts.

Plain language in and out

Ask like a person, read like a person. No query syntax, no wall of jargon in the reply.

Dashboards that lead somewhere

A number is a starting point. Every metric opens into the detail and the source behind it.

Graceful when it does not know

An uncertain answer says so plainly and points to a next step, rather than inventing something.

One coherent product

Chat and dashboards share the same language, so moving between them never feels like two tools.

Why it matters for Cigna

Trustworthy AI is the whole game in healthcare

Everything this project is about, showing sources, being honest about certainty, designing so a person can verify rather than simply believe, is exactly what AI needs in a regulated, high-stakes world. Designing AI that earns trust, across both a conversational surface and dashboards, is precisely the kind of experience an AI programme on the Cigna side will need to get right. 04  What I bring

The range I can lead across.

User research

I do not guess at what people need, I go and find out. Interviews, watching people use the real thing, testing early while it is still cheap to change. The aim is to be surprised in a research session, not after launch. Some of my best decisions started as something a user did that I did not expect.

Service design

A screen is only ever part of someone's experience. I map the whole journey, the moments before and after the interface, the handoffs, the places where people quietly fall through the cracks. Fixing the screen rarely fixes the experience. Designing the service around it does.

Design thinking, facilitated

The best idea in the room often never gets said unless someone makes space for it. I run design thinking sessions that pull engineers, business and users into the same problem, so what we build is something the whole team believes in, not something design handed down.

05  For this engagement

How I would lead the overall experience.

As the experience lead I would own one thing above all, the way the whole thing feels to the people who use it, no matter how many teams are building the parts. The POC teams do brilliant work in their own areas. My job is to make sure those parts add up to something that feels like one product, not a collection of clever pieces.

In practice that means holding the map of the end to end journey, setting the design language and the patterns everyone works from, sitting close to each POC team so decisions stay aligned, and keeping the person we are building for at the centre of every conversation. People first, technology second, all the way through.

06  About

A designer who started in anthropology.

I am Sai Keerthi Koppolu, a UI and UX lead with about twelve years designing and shipping software. I started out designing self-service for NCR, the ATMs and kiosks people use in public without a second thought, which is where I learned that the hardest audience to design for is everyone. These days I lead experience design for enterprise AI at Accenture, most recently on the healthcare side.

My training began in anthropology, and I am still studying it. That is not a footnote. Anthropology is the study of how people actually behave, in their real context, rather than how a specification wishes they would. It is the lens I bring to every product, and the reason my instinct is always people first, technology second.

Outside of work I write. My first book, Astitva, Living Beyond Pain, is published, and I am working on a second about staying human in the age of AI. The thread across all of it, the kiosks, the AI, the writing, is one question. How do we build technology that respects the person on the other side of it.

"I do not believe UX ends at the screen. The best experiences happen when the interface, the people, the environment and the process behind it all work together."
